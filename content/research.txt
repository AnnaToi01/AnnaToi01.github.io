HOW THIS FILE WORKS
All the text in the pop-up windows on the Research page (projects.html) comes
from this file. Edit it, save, and reload the page.

  === name ===        starts a window. Keep the names as they are, they connect
                      each window to its drawing:
                        intro    - the "Made by" stamp
                        phd      - the laptop
                        plasmids - the whiteboard
                        gfp      - the jellyfish
                        mice     - the mouse
  file: name.txt      the file name shown in the title bar and the C:\> line
  # Title [now]       the window's title; [now] or [previously] adds the small
                      coloured tag (leave it out for no tag)
  ## Heading [now]    a smaller heading inside the window
  ---                 a thin dividing line
  > text              a small grey note
  *text*              italic,  **text** bold,  [text](https://...) a link

Paragraphs are separated by an empty line. Line breaks inside a paragraph
don't matter. Everything above the first === line is ignored.

# Co-evolution of MGEs [now]

I am currently pursuing a PhD at Institut Pasteur and Collège de France co-supervised by Eduardo
Rocha and François Blanquart. I study how mobile genetic elements (MGEs), like
plasmids or phages, shape the evolution of bacteria.

These elements are often thought of as parasites of their hosts: plasmids may
incur costs for their maintenance and transfer, while phages may even kill the
cells they infect. But, at the same time, these MGEs may be useful to their
hosts, as they carry traits such as antimicrobial resistance or virulence.

Even more interestingly, these elements can themselves have parasites. Some
plasmids hijack the transfer machinery of other plasmids to spread, while phage
satellites hijack phage particles for their own dissemination. This encapsulates
interactions that range from parasitism to mutualism and unfold over several
nested host-parasite interactions within one bacterial genome.

I am curious about how these MGEs influence one another and their
bacterial hosts, sometimes creating systems composed of parasites of parasites of
parasites. I want to understand: how do these parasites and their hosts co-evolve,
and what changes when we move beyond the usual two-player host–parasite system?

---

## Pangenome evolution of *E. coli* [now]

*Escherichia coli* is one of the best-studied organisms in biology and it is dual in its nature. On one hand, it is a common gut commensal found in humans, farm animals, and wildlife. On another hand, it is a major opportunistic pathogen responsible for up to a million deaths each year due intra- and extraintestinal infections.

Pathogenicity of *E. coli* partly depends on its genome composition and evolutionary history. Some phylogenetic groups, like B2 and D, are more common in infections than others. I study the pangenome evolution of major successful clonal lineages, sequence types, of *E. coli*. All of them are globally widespread and can be found both
in healthy carriers and infected patients, but some are much more strongly
associated with infections than others. I wanted to understand: what makes more
pathogenic clones different from more commensal ones? What makes some
*E. coli* lineages more successful than others? How do their pangenomes
diversify over time? And what drives their diversification? (wink at the MGEs
above)

I’ve presented this work at MEEHubs2026 and “Ecology and evolution of
host-parasite interactions in a changing world” Jacques Monod Conference in
Roscoff.

# Mathematical modeling of plasmids [previously]

Some plasmids encode the machinery needed to make a plus and transfer themselves between
bacterial cells and they are called conjugative plasmids. Others, called mobilizable
plasmids, do not encode this costly machinery and instead hijack it from
conjugative plasmids for horizontal transfer. I created a mathematical model to
understand: under which conditions can these elements coexist? How do factors
such as transfer rates, plasmid costs, and segregation loss shape whether their
relationship looks more like parasitism or mutualism? I also added traits such as
antimicrobial resistance and toxin-antitoxin systems, often carried by plasmids,
to ask whether they shift the dynamics of the whole system in one direction or
the other.

# GFP fitness landscapes [previously]

How do we map a protein sequence to its function? This
relationship can be represented using fitness landscapes. Using green fluorescent protein (GFP) as a model system and their fluorescence
as the measure of fitness, I studied how these landscapes differ between closely
related proteins. I was curious about how mutations interact with one another through epistasis
and how these interactions shape protein function and evolution. 

We asked: why are some proteins more tolerant to mutations than
others? Can their biophysical properties predict this mutational robustness? How
transferable are the effects of mutations between similar proteins? And how
should fitness landscapes be explored experimentally and through machine learning
to best predict protein function and guide protein design?

With the help of the Young Investigator Travel and Undergraduate Travel Awards,
I presented this work at SMBE 2025 in Beijing.

# Subretinal fibrosis [previously]

Fibrosis develops when the normal process of scar formation becomes
dysregulated, causing excessive scar tissue to build up and interfere with how an
organ functions. In the eye, subretinal fibrosis can develop as an end stage of
neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), a major cause of central
vision loss in people over 50.

During my internship at Roche, I analysed single-nucleus RNA-seq data from three
different mouse models to understand: how do these models differ from one
another, and how well do they reflect the human disease? What gene-expression
signatures characterize each model, and how well do they translate to humans? And
how do ageing and different anti-permeability treatments influence the
development of subretinal fibrosis?
