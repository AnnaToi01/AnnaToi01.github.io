Co-evolution of MGEs

I am currently pursuing a PhD at Institut Pasteur co-supervised by Eduardo Rocha and François Blanquart. I study how mobile genetic elements (MGEs), like plasmids or phages, shape the evolution of bacteria.

These elements are often thought of as parasites of their hosts: plasmids may incur costs for their maintenance and transfer, while phages may even lyse the cells they infect. But, at the same time, these MGEs may be useful to their hosts, as they carry traits such as antimicrobial resistance or virulence.

Even more interestingly, these elements can themselves have parasites. Some plasmids hijack the transfer machinery of other plasmids to spread, while phage satellites hijack phage particles for their own dissemination. This encapsulates interactions that range from parasitism to mutualism and unfold over several nested levels within one bacterial genome.

I am curious about how these MGEs interact with one another and with their bacterial hosts, sometimes creating systems composed of parasites of parasites of parasites. I want to understand: how do these parasites and their hosts co-evolve, and what changes when we move beyond the usual two-player host–parasite system?

Pangenome evolution of E. coli

Escherichia coli is one of the best-studied organisms in biology, but it has a dual nature: it is a common gut commensal, while also being a major opportunistic pathogen responsible for up to a million deaths each year.

Whether an E. coli is more likely to live harmlessly in the gut or cause disease depends partly on its genomic composition and evolutionary history. In my master's thesis I studied the pangenome evolution of major clones, sequence types, of E. coli. All of them are globally widespread and can be found both in healthy carriers and infected patients, but some are much more strongly associated with infections than others. I wanted to understand: what makes more pathogenic clones different from more commensal ones? What makes some E. coli lineages more successful than others? How do their pangenomes diversify over time? And what drives their diversification? (wink at the MGEs above)

I’ve presented this work at MEEHubs2026 and “Ecology and evolution of host–parasite interactions in a changing world” Jacques Monod Conference in Roscoff.

Mathematical modeling of plasmids

Some plasmids encode the machinery needed to transfer themselves between bacterial cells and are called conjugative plasmids. Others, called mobilizable plasmids, do not encode this costly machinery and instead hijack it from conjugative plasmids for horizontal transfer. I created a mathematical model to understand: under which conditions can these elements coexist? How do factors such as transfer rates, plasmid costs, and segregation loss shape whether their relationship looks more like parasitism or mutualism? I also added traits such as antimicrobial resistance and toxin-antitoxin systems, often carried by plasmids, to ask whether they shift the dynamics of the whole system in one direction or the other.

GFP fitness landscapes

I was curious about how mutations interact with one another through epistasis and how these interactions shape protein function and evolution. This relationship can be represented using fitness landscapes, where protein sequences are mapped to their function.

Using green fluorescent protein (GFP) as a model system and their fluorescence as the measure of fitness, I studied how these landscapes differ between closely related proteins. We asked: why are some proteins more tolerant to mutations than others? Can their biophysical properties predict this mutational robustness? How transferable are the effects of mutations between similar proteins? And how should fitness landscapes be explored experimentally and through machine learning to best predict protein function and guide protein design?

With the help of the Young Investigator Travel and Undergraduate Travel Awards, I presented this work at SMBE 2025 in Beijing.

Subretinal fibrosis

Fibrosis develops when the normal process of scar formation becomes dysregulated, causing excessive scar tissue to build up and interfere with how an organ functions. In the eye, subretinal fibrosis can develop as an end stage of neovascular age-related macular degeneration (nAMD), a major cause of central vision loss in people over 50.

During my internship at Roche, I analysed single-nucleus RNA-seq data from three different mouse models to understand: how do these models differ from one another, and how well do they reflect the human disease? What gene-expression signatures characterize each model, and how well do they translate to humans? And how do ageing and different anti-permeability treatments influence the development of subretinal fibrosis?
